# Common and relevant food allergens in Cambodia and Asia

Date: 2026-09-08

## Decision summary

There is not enough public clinical evidence to rank food allergens specifically for Cambodia. The most defensible Phase 1 decision is therefore to use a clearly labelled Southeast Asian and broader Asian evidence base, then validate it with Cambodian clinical experts and local package-label data.

For an ingredient-checking dataset, use this provisional category set:

| Coverage tier | Allergen group | Evidence-based reason to include | Confidence for Asian relevance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Core | Crustaceans | Shrimp, prawn, and crab repeatedly lead Southeast Asian population surveys and are prominent severe-reaction triggers, especially after early childhood. | High |
| Core | Fish | Repeatedly follows crustaceans in Vietnamese population data and appears in Thai challenge-confirmed cases. Keep it separate from shellfish. | Moderate-high |
| Core | Egg | One of the leading challenge-confirmed and severe-reaction triggers in Asian infants and young children. | High for young children |
| Core | Milk | One of the leading challenge-confirmed and severe-reaction triggers in Asian infants and young children. | High for young children |
| Core | Wheat | Appears in challenge-confirmed Thai cases and is an important anaphylaxis trigger in Thailand, Japan, and Korea. Wheat allergy must remain distinct from coeliac disease and non-coeliac gluten sensitivity. | Moderate-high |
| Core | Peanut | Population prevalence is comparatively low in several older Southeast Asian surveys, but peanut is a significant severe-reaction trigger in Singapore, Hong Kong, and Korea. | Moderate; geographically variable |
| Core | Tree nuts | Nut-triggered anaphylaxis is prominent in Singapore and Hong Kong, while walnut is important in Korean pediatric data. Store individual nuts rather than assuming all tree nuts are interchangeable. | Moderate-high; geographically variable |
| Supporting core | Molluscs | Vietnamese child and adult surveys identify molluscs separately from crustaceans. They should not be collapsed into one undifferentiated “shellfish” group. | Moderate |
| Supporting core | Soy | Clinical allergy is less frequent than sensitization in the available Southeast Asian studies, but soy remains relevant to younger children and Asian packaged foods. | Low-moderate |
| Supporting core | Sesame | Direct Cambodia and Southeast Asia prevalence evidence is sparse, but sesame is on the current Codex global priority list and should be covered in an internationally compatible system. | Low for regional frequency; high regulatory relevance |

This is a **coverage priority**, not a numerical prevalence ranking and not a safety assessment. “Asia” is too heterogeneous for a single universal order, and the leading foods change with age, geography, study method, and whether the outcome is any clinical allergy or only anaphylaxis.

## How evidence was interpreted

The evidence types answer different questions and must remain separate:

| Evidence type | What it supports | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Population study with oral food challenge (OFC) | Best available estimate of clinical food allergy in the sampled population | Expensive, uncommon, and often small |
| Convincing clinical history plus skin-prick test or serum-specific IgE | Probable clinical allergy | Still not equivalent to challenge confirmation |
| Structured self- or parent-report | Which foods people associate with reactions | Usually overestimates clinical allergy and depends on recognition and access to diagnosis |
| Skin-prick test or serum-specific IgE alone | Sensitization | Sensitization does not prove symptoms on eating the food |
| Hospital or anaphylaxis registry | Which foods cause medically important or severe reactions among recorded cases | Referral and case-ascertainment bias; not population prevalence |

The joint FAO/WHO expert consultation likewise treats challenge-supported evidence as more reliable than self-report and evaluates priority allergens using prevalence, potency, and severity rather than prevalence alone. [FAO/WHO risk assessment of priority food allergens](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240042391)

## Cambodia-specific evidence

### Explicit data gap

No public Cambodian population prevalence study, oral-food-challenge study, food-allergy registry, or participant-level dataset was located that can rank individual allergen groups.

- A 2013 review searched for food-allergy literature by individual Asian country, explicitly including Cambodia, but reported evidence from other Asian countries rather than a Cambodian study. Its literature window ended in December 2012. [Lee, Thalayasingam, and Lee, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3563019/)
- A 2024 Asia-Pacific review describes the regional evidence as limited, notes that most available studies are self-reported, and tabulates no Cambodian prevalence study. [Leung et al., 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/pai.14211)
- A 2018 Cambodian consumer survey found that four of 152 shoppers said allergy affecting themselves or a family member influenced purchasing, but it did not identify the foods, verify diagnosis, or estimate population prevalence. It cannot establish a Cambodian allergen ranking. [CIRD and Consumers International survey](https://www.cird.org.kh/images/Food%20Standards%20Consumer%20Survey%20Report-CI-June%202018.pdf)

Therefore, statements such as “shrimp is the most common food allergen in Cambodia” are not currently supported by the public evidence reviewed here. The correct product language is: **Cambodia-specific evidence is unavailable; the initial coverage set is based on Southeast Asian and broader Asian evidence.**

## Southeast Asian evidence

### Clinical allergy and population evidence

| Study | Method and population | Result | What it supports |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Northern Thailand preschool surveys, 2010 and 2019 | Two population-based cross-sectional studies of children aged 3–7 using questionnaire, skin-prick/specific-IgE testing, and OFC | Parent-reported food allergy fell from 9.3% to 5.5%, while OFC-confirmed allergy was 1.1% and 0.9%. In the 2019 reports, milk, shrimp, and egg accounted for 47%, 33%, and 18% of named foods. Confirmed cases across the study included shrimp or giant river prawn, tilapia, milk, and wheat. | Strong direct evidence for crustacean coverage, plus fish, milk, egg, and wheat; also demonstrates how self-report exceeds challenge confirmation. [Rangkakulnuwat et al., 2021](https://www.worldallergyorganizationjournal.org/article/S1939-4551%2821%2900087-9/fulltext) |
| Vietnam preschool survey | Population-based questionnaire completed for 8,620 children aged 2–6 | Among 580 reported food-allergy cases, crustaceans accounted for 330 (56.9%), followed by fish, molluscs, beef, milk, and egg. | Large regional signal for crustaceans and support for keeping fish and molluscs separate; still self-/parent-reported rather than OFC-confirmed. [Le et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30793379/) |
| Singapore and Philippines school survey | Structured symptom-history questionnaire; 23,425 children within the study age groups | Convincing shellfish allergy was estimated at 1.19% in Singapore children aged 4–6, 5.23% in Singapore adolescents, and 5.12% in Philippine adolescents. Peanut was 0.43%–0.64% and tree nuts 0.28%–0.33% in the local groups. | Strong evidence that shellfish becomes especially important in older Southeast Asian children; peanut and tree-nut frequency was lower in these populations. Not challenge-confirmed. [Shek et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20624649/) |
| Vietnam adult survey | National population-based questionnaire | Doctor-diagnosed allergy was reported most often for crustaceans (3.0%), then fish (1.6%), molluscs (1.3%), and beef (1.0%). | Adult evidence for the same seafood pattern, subject to self-report and access-to-diagnosis limitations. [Le et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32161634/) |
| Malaysian infant cohort | 314 infants tested at 12 months; immediate symptoms plus specific IgE used for the clinical-allergy definition | Sensitization was highest for beef (14.3%), peanut (10.8%), and egg white (7.0%), but defined IgE-mediated food allergy was egg 3.2%, milk 1.0%, wheat 0.6%, and soy 0.3%. | Egg and milk are clinically important in infancy; the gap between sensitization and clinical allergy is substantial. No OFC confirmation. [Yong et al., 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/12/8/2418) |

### Severe-reaction and referral evidence

- The prospective Asia-Pacific Research Network for Anaphylaxis recorded 721 pediatric anaphylaxis episodes at 16 centers in Thailand, Singapore, Hong Kong, and Qingdao from 2019–2022. Among food-induced episodes, egg (38%) and milk (27%) led under age three; tree nuts led ages four to six (31%); and shellfish led ages seven to eleven (37%) and twelve to seventeen (44%). Wheat was the leading trigger in Bangkok, whereas nuts were concentrated in Singapore and Hong Kong. These figures describe **anaphylaxis cases**, not general-population prevalence. [Leung et al., 2024](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/all.16098)
- In a Thai adult allergy-clinic cohort, shellfish accounted for 68.0% and wheat for 28.7% of identified food triggers; 85.3% of shellfish-reactive patients reported shrimp. This supports adult/severity coverage but has strong referral bias. [Thai adult food-allergy study, 2023](https://apjai-journal.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/AP-210223-1548.pdf)

### Southeast Asian synthesis

The consistent pattern is:

1. **Crustaceans are the clearest regional priority**, especially shrimp/prawn and crab.
2. **Fish and molluscs are recurrent but distinct seafood groups.**
3. **Egg and milk are especially important in infancy and early childhood.**
4. **Wheat is important because it appears in confirmed Thai cases and severe-reaction data even where general-population estimates are limited.**
5. **Peanut and tree nuts may be less frequent in some local prevalence surveys but remain important severe triggers in more urbanized Asian settings.**
6. **Soy evidence is weaker for clinical allergy than for sensitization.**

## Broader Asian evidence

Broader Asian evidence is useful for coverage, but it should not be presented as Cambodian prevalence.

| Geography and study | Evidence type | Findings | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chongqing, China, infants | Population screening followed by OFC | Among 477 infants completing the study, challenge-confirmed food allergy was 3.8%: egg 2.5% and milk 1.3%. | Strong challenge-confirmed support for egg and milk in infancy. [Hu et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21265885/) |
| Korea, children and adolescents | Multicenter clinical case series of 1,661 immediate-type food-allergy cases | Milk (28.1%) and egg (27.6%) led overall; wheat (7.9%), walnut (7.3%), and peanut (5.3%) followed. Walnut led in preschool and school-aged groups, while buckwheat led among adolescents. Of 506 anaphylaxis cases, milk, egg, wheat, walnut, peanut, buckwheat, and pine nut were the leading foods. | Supports tree nuts and wheat in the core and buckwheat/pine nut as regional extensions. Clinical case mix is not population prevalence. [Jeong et al., 2017](https://e-aair.org/pdf/10.4168/aair.2017.9.5.423) |
| Korea, all ages | Multicenter anaphylaxis registry of 558 cases | Food caused 62.2% of recorded anaphylaxis. In children, egg led, followed by milk, walnut, wheat, and peanut; in adults, shrimp and wheat were prominent food triggers. | Corroborates the age-dependent shift from egg/milk to seafood/wheat and the importance of walnut. Registry evidence, not prevalence. [Yang et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7426446/) |
| China | Meta-analysis of population-based studies | Commonly reported foods were mango (1.9%), shrimp (1.5%), egg (1.4%), milk (1.3%), and crab (1.3%), with substantial regional and methodological heterogeneity. | Supports mango as an extension and corroborates shrimp, egg, milk, and crab; pooled estimates should not be transferred to Cambodia. [Feng et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37641218/) |

## Regional extensions to track separately

These should be represented as candidate extensions rather than silently folded into the core:

| Candidate | Why track it | Current confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buckwheat | Important cause of Korean and Japanese allergy/anaphylaxis and the leading food in Korean adolescents in one multicenter clinical study. | Moderate for Northeast Asia; low for Cambodia |
| Pine nut | Appears among leading Korean pediatric anaphylaxis triggers but is not interchangeable with every regulatory tree-nut list. | Moderate for Korea; low for Southeast Asia |
| Mango and other fruits | Mango appears prominently in Chinese population syntheses; fruit patterns vary substantially by geography. | Moderate for parts of China; low for Cambodia |
| Beef/red meat | Appears in Vietnamese surveys and Malaysian sensitization data; Sri Lankan clinic data also identify meat/milk-associated anaphylaxis. | Low-moderate; method-sensitive |
| Chickpea and other legumes | Reported as important in India, reflecting regional diets; do not infer that all legumes belong to peanut or soy groups. | Moderate for South Asia; low for Cambodia |
| Bird's nest and galacto-oligosaccharide-containing formula | Documented as unusual Southeast Asian triggers, including severe reactions, but not common enough to merge into broad core groups. | Low-frequency, high-specificity signal |

The 2013 Asian review summarizes these geographic differences and identifies shellfish as the leading allergy in older children and adults in much of Southeast Asia, with egg and milk more prominent in young children and region-specific foods elsewhere. [Lee, Thalayasingam, and Lee, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3563019/)

## Relationship to international priority lists

The current Codex labelling standard is a useful compatibility check, not a substitute for Asian epidemiology. Its global declaration list includes wheat, rye, barley, crustacea, egg, fish, peanut, milk, sesame, and six specified tree nuts. It also separates candidates that national or regional authorities may require, including buckwheat, oats, soy, Brazil nut, macadamia, and pine nut. [Codex CXS 1-1985](https://www.fao.org/fao-who-codexalimentarius/sh-proxy/fr/?lnk=1&url=https%3A%2F%2Fworkspace.fao.org%2Fsites%2Fcodex%2FStandards%2FCXS+1-1985%2FCXS_001e.pdf)

For the future dataset, keep two independent attributes:

- `regional_evidence_priority`: derived from the clinical evidence above; and
- `regulatory_status`: derived from Codex and the applicable national law.

This prevents a legally required declaration category from being misrepresented as “the most common allergy in Cambodia,” and prevents a clinically relevant regional food from being omitted merely because it is not on a global list.

## Recommended Phase 1 output

Adopt the ten groups in the decision table as the first coverage target, but do not assign them a single numeric rank. Instead, tag evidence by:

- geography: `cambodia`, `southeast_asia`, or `broader_asia`;
- age band;
- outcome: `challenge_confirmed_allergy`, `probable_clinical_allergy`, `self_reported_allergy`, `sensitization`, or `anaphylaxis`;
- study setting: population, clinic, or registry; and
- confidence and source.

Before calling the list Cambodia-specific, obtain at least one Cambodian study using a defined clinical case definition, ideally with OFC confirmation in a population sample. Until then, the list must remain a regional proxy and must not be used to produce allergen-free, safety, or medical verdicts.
